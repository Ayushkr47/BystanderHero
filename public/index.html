An ambulance takes 4 to 8 minutes. Survival from cardiac arrest falls about 10% for every minute nobody acts. The people who could act are already standing there — they just don't know what to do, or assume someone else will.

BystanderHero listens to the scene, works out what kind of emergency it is, and gives every person present a different job — synchronised live, on every phone at once.

How it works

Four people, four jobs, one clock.

01 · Hear

It listens to the shouting

Speech from the scene goes to Claude, which picks one protocol out of five and reports how confident it is. Under 60% confidence, or any time you disagree, a human confirms first. No network? A cue engine on the server does the same job offline.

02 · Split

It splits the work

The bystander effect is a coordination failure, not apathy. So nobody chooses. Each arriving phone is handed the next unfilled role in survival order — compressions, the 911 call, the AED, crowd control, relief.

03 · Sync

Everyone sees the same scene

One shared session over server-sent events. When the AED runner ticks "AED attached", it appears on the compressor's phone instantly. One clock, one checklist, no duplicated effort, no gaps.

04 · Sustain

It keeps the quality up

A 110 bpm visual metronome holds the compression rate. Relief rotation is prompted every two minutes and swaps roles across phones in one tap, because tired compressions stop working long before the person doing them notices.

Where this stops

Designed to defer.

The dispatcher outranks the app. Every protocol says so, on screen, in the role that holds the phone call. If 911 says something different, 911 wins.

It never diagnoses. The AI picks between five published lay-rescuer protocols and nothing else. It cannot invent an instruction, because every instruction is written in advance and reviewed — the model only routes.

A human can always override. Low confidence blocks dispatch until someone confirms, and any responder can switch protocol mid-incident, which rebriefs the whole scene.

It assumes untrained hands. Guidance stays inside what a layperson can safely do: no blind finger sweeps, no restraining a seizure, no lifting a dressing to check a bleed.
